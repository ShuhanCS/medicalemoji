# ACEP Supports Standardized Medical Emoji for Clearer Health Communication

*Why the College is backing an effort to bring the language of medicine to the
emoji keyboard on every phone.*

Emergency physicians communicate under time pressure, often with patients who
speak different languages, read at different levels, and arrive in moments of
crisis. In that environment, a clear visual anchor can do real work. That is the
premise behind the [Medical Emoji Project](https://medicalemoji.org), an effort to
add high-utility medical symbols to the Unicode Standard — the global system behind
the emoji that appear on virtually every smartphone, tablet, and computer. The
American College of Emergency Physicians has now issued a [formal letter of
support](https://medicalemoji.org/documents/ACEP-Medical-Emoji-Support-Letter-2026.pdf)
for that work.

## A communication layer, not a replacement

Standardized medical emoji are meant to add a familiar, globally available layer for
patient education, discharge instructions, public health messaging, medical
education, and informal patient–provider communication. They are not a substitute
for clinical language or professional interpretation. The value is in giving common
clinical concepts — organ systems, infection, cardiac monitoring, transfusion,
medication use, and weight-based care — a simple, shared symbol that travels across
languages, literacy levels, ages, and disabilities.

For the informatics community, the appeal is structural. Emoji are already a
standardized, cross-platform character set with stable code points and broad
rendering support. Extending that set toward clinical concepts means a consistent
visual vocabulary that downstream systems — messaging, patient portals, education
materials — can rely on without bespoke iconography.

## The approach already has a proven precedent

This is not a hypothetical. In 2019, emergency physician [Shuhan He,
MD](https://medicalemoji.org) and collaborators helped submit the anatomical heart
and lungs to Unicode. Both were accepted into Unicode 14.0 and shipped on billions
of devices in 2021, and the effort was documented in *JAMA*. That success showed the
medical community can successfully navigate a rigorous international standards process
— and it set the template for what comes next.

## Ten concepts now in development

ACEP's letter names ten concepts the project is pursuing, each tied to a substantial
clinical and public-health footprint:

- **Liver** — liver disease causes roughly 2 million deaths a year worldwide.
- **Kidney** — chronic kidney disease affects about 10% of the global population.
- **Stomach** — GERD carries an estimated $18 billion in direct annual costs.
- **Intestine / bowel** — colorectal cancer is the second-leading cause of cancer death.
- **White blood cell** — a symbol for infection and immunity, from everyday illness to pandemics.
- **[ECG / EKG](https://medicalemoji.org/emoji/ekg)** — one person dies of cardiovascular disease every 36 seconds in the U.S.
- **Spine** — low back pain is the leading cause of years lived with disability worldwide.
- **Blood product** — in the U.S., someone needs blood roughly every two seconds.
- **Pill pack** — a marker for medication use and adherence amid the opioid crisis.
- **Weight scale** — more than 2 billion people worldwide live with overweight or obesity.

A complete view of each concept, along with the [full list of supporting
organizations](https://medicalemoji.org/endorsements), is available on the project's
site.

## How the standards process works

Unicode evaluates emoji proposals through a formal, published process. Each proposal
must satisfy criteria including distinctiveness, expected frequency of use, image
licensing, and broad applicability. Professional and clinical endorsements speak
directly to that last point: they help demonstrate that a proposed symbol carries
real-world relevance across a wide population. Petitions and awareness campaigns, by
contrast, are explicitly not counted as usage evidence — which is part of why a
society's measured, professional support matters.

## What ACEP's support adds

ACEP's endorsement is clinical and professional. It reflects a judgment that these
concepts are relevant to emergency care and could help clinicians and patients
communicate more effectively in modern digital environments. As a national,
multi-specialty body, the College's support speaks to the broad applicability that
the standards process weighs — and signals that the goal is better representation of
medicine and health in the tools people already use every day.
