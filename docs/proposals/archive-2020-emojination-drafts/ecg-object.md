# **Abstract**

This proposal requests the addition of an ECG (OBJECT) emoji. The “ECG”
stands for electrocardiography, a graph of voltage versus time of the
electrical activity of the heart using electrodes placed on the skin
indicating leads. This ultimately enables the physician to check for
signs of heart disease.

An ECG is classically known by the layman to see if there is a
myocardial infarction (or heart attack). However, other key diagnoses
can involve poor blood flow (ischemia), heart rhythm, abnormal wall
thickening, electrolyte abnormalities, etc.

This highlights an important opportunity to improve public health by
making available an ECG emoji for digital communication.

# Introduction

In the United States, the number of adults with diagnosed heart disease
is 30.3 million. The percentage of adults diagnosed with heart disease
is 12.1%. In fact, cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading
cause of death in the United States, responsible for 840,768 deaths in
2016 according to the American Heart Association (AHA). Specifically,
somebody has a heart attack every 40 seconds. Every year, about 805,000
Americans have a heart attack, and of these, 605,000 are a first heart
attack according to the Center for Disease Control (CDC).

Many cases of CVD include risk factors such as diabetes, prior coronary
disease, peripheral artery disease, chronic kidney disease,
hypertension, hyperlipidemia, family history, etc. However, some
modifiable risk factors involve smoking, obesity, and sedentary
lifestyle.

At the forefront of diagnosis of all this are ECGs. They may be used to
clinically diagnose patients with chest pain, rapid heart beat,
arrhythmia, shortness of breath, dizziness, and fatigue. Early
identification and diagnosis is critical in the management of patients
with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and other heart issues in the
emergency department. It is further the most widely used initial
screening test for evaluating patients with chest pain. Additionally, it
is the most important initial tool for patients with suspected ACS
because it is inexpensive available and non0-invasive. The ECG is (and
should be) the first diagnostic test that is performed on patients who
present to the emergency department with chest pain or angina symptoms.
ECG changes of ischemia occur before infarction (heart attack),
providing the ability to itnervent to restore blood flow before
myocardial cell death occurs. In fact, early detection is so critical
that the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association
(ACC/AHA) guidelines specify that an ECG should be obtained and
interpreted within 10 minutes of arrival to the emergency department in
patients with symptoms indicating ACS.
