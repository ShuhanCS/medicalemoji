That L2 route is the right vehicle for the broader issue because the individual emoji form is built for one character at a time. It is not designed to ask how Unicode should evaluate a related class of health and medical concepts, how reviewers should compare them with existing substitutes, or whether their expected usage is being judged consistently. A UTC/L2 paper creates a concise record for that process question and gives UTC a procedural action it can take: refer the issue to the Emoji Standard & Research Working Group for review and recommendation. The paper does not ask Unicode to encode a bundle of emoji, and it would not replace the individual submission form. Unicode assigns an official L2 number after a document is accepted into its public register, so the attached paper does not use a placeholder number.

There is useful history behind that approach. Apple's 2018 L2/18-080 presented nine accessibility emoji as a finite starting set and explained how the related concepts fit together. In 2019, Christian Kamkoff, Shuhan He, and Melissa Thermidor submitted separate proposals for Anatomical Heart and Lungs; Unicode later encoded both. The lesson I take from those examples is that related context can be helpful, but each character still needs an independent case.
