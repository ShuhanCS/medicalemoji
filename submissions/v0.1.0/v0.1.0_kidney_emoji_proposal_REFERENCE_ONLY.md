## 3. Factors For Inclusion

### A. Multiple Concepts

The kidney emoji would represent a broad, durable concept rather than a single disease, procedure, or awareness campaign. It can be used for:

- Human anatomy and organ identity.
- Kidney health and kidney function.
- Filtration, urine production, hydration, electrolyte balance, and medication safety.
- Chronic kidney disease and kidney failure.
- Dialysis and kidney replacement therapy.
- Kidney transplant and kidney donation.
- Kidney stones and acute kidney pain.
- Acute kidney injury.
- Diabetes and hypertension complications affecting kidney health.
- Public-health messaging about early detection and access to care.

The kidney is also a common everyday word outside specialist nephrology, including public discussion of kidney stones, kidney donation, kidney transplant, kidney failure, dialysis, kidney health, and kidney disease.

Evidence still needed:

- Required frequency screenshots listed in Section 3E.
- Final search/result counts for `kidney`.
- Supplemental usage evidence for `kidney disease`, `kidney transplant`, `kidney stone`, and `dialysis`.

### B. Use In Sequences

The kidney emoji would combine naturally with existing emoji to convey additional concepts:

- Kidney + water drop: hydration, urine, filtration.
- Kidney + hospital: kidney care, nephrology visit, hospitalization for kidney disease.
- Kidney + pill: medication safety, kidney-protective medication, nephrotoxic medications.
- Kidney + syringe: labs, biopsy, treatment, transplant-related care.
- Kidney + warning: acute kidney injury, urgent symptoms, abnormal labs.
- Kidney + person: donor, recipient, patient, clinician discussion.
- Kidney + blue circle/ribbon: kidney awareness context when appropriate.

These are examples of ordinary emoji composition and are not proposed as ZWJ sequences.

### C. Breaks New Ground

Kidney would add a high-utility internal organ that is not represented by current emoji. The concept is visually and semantically distinct from existing anatomy emoji and from food emoji such as beans. It is not merely a variant of an existing emoji.

The kidney has a specific public-health, transplant, donation, dialysis, stone, and medication-safety role that cannot be cleanly conveyed by the anatomical heart, lungs, brain, hospital, pill, drop, or bean emoji. Existing emoji can support kidney-related communication in sequences, but none directly represents the organ or kidney-specific disease and care contexts.

This proposal does not rely on the argument that heart, brain, or lungs exist. Those emoji are context only. The kidney proposal stands on independent usage, distinctiveness, and broad medical and everyday meaning.

### D. Distinctiveness

The kidney has a recognizable bean-shaped anatomical silhouette and commonly appears as a paired organ in educational and clinical materials. That recognizability also creates the main design risk: the image must not collapse into a generic bean at `18x18`.

The final proposal should include:

- `18x18` and `72x72` renderings.
- Side-by-side checks against bean emoji and existing organ emoji.
- A note explaining how the final art distinguishes kidney anatomy from beans.
- A small-size readability review by people who are not nephrology specialists.

### F. Completeness

Kidney helps fill a practical gap in medical and public-health emoji communication. The argument should be narrow: kidney is not included simply to complete an unlimited list of body organs. Kidney has independent expected usage because of kidney disease burden, kidney failure, dialysis, transplantation, donation, stones, acute kidney injury, medication safety, and global public-health relevance.

### G. Compatibility

No compatibility evidence is currently claimed. Mark as not applicable unless a high-usage kidney pictograph is found in a major existing communication system and can be documented.

## 4. Factors For Exclusion

### A. Already Represented

No current emoji directly represents a kidney. Existing emoji do not adequately cover kidney anatomy, kidney disease, kidney donation, kidney transplant, dialysis, kidney stones, acute kidney injury, or kidney-specific care communication.

Beans are not an adequate substitute. In clinical, patient-facing, donation, transplant, and public-health contexts, using a bean emoji can be confusing or unserious and does not clearly represent a human organ.

### B. Overly Specific

Kidney is not overly specific. It is a primary organ and a broad health concept used in everyday language and clinical communication. It is not limited to a single diagnosis, procedure, profession, event, or organization.

### C. Open-Ended

The proposal does not ask Unicode to encode every organ. Kidney is proposed because it has an independent and broad use case across common public-health, patient, transplant, donation, dialysis, acute-care, and medication-safety contexts.

### D. Transient

Kidney health, kidney disease, kidney stones, kidney failure, dialysis, transplant, donation, diabetes, hypertension, and medication safety are enduring topics. The proposal is not tied to a temporary campaign or trend.

### E. Faulty Comparison

The kidney emoji is not justified only because anatomical heart, lungs, or brain emoji exist. The proposal addresses kidney's own frequency, multiple uses, distinctiveness, and broad public-health relevance.

## 5. Other Information

### Current Health And Public-Health Context

The final proposal should cite current sources carefully and avoid outdated claims.

WHO kidney disease fact sheet:
https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/kidney-disease

Key current facts from WHO:

- WHO estimates that 674 million people have chronic kidney disease worldwide.
- Kidney failure requires dialysis or kidney transplantation to sustain life.
- Kidney disease is often asymptomatic until late stages.
- The burden is rising with diabetes, hypertension, and population ageing.

Lancet / GBD 2023 Chronic Kidney Disease Collaborators:
https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(25)01853-7

Key current facts from the GBD analysis:

- In 2023, 788 million adults aged 20 years and older were estimated to have CKD.
- Global age-standardized adult CKD prevalence was 14.2%.
- CKD was the ninth leading cause of death globally in 2023.
- CKD accounted for 1.48 million deaths globally in 2023.

CDC chronic kidney disease data:
https://www.cdc.gov/kidney-disease/php/data-research/index.html

Key current facts from CDC:

- More than 1 in 10 US adults aged 18 or older, about 14%, were estimated to have CKD.
- This corresponds to about 37 million people.
- About 9 in 10 adults aged 20 or older with CKD did not know they had CKD.
